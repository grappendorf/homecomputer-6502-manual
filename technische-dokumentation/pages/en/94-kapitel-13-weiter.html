The computer runs. This chapter sums up what you have built, shows how the breadboard circuit became the finished device, and offers ideas, help and sources for the next step.

The Big Picture

The 6502 processor talks to all chips over the address bus (16 lines) and the data bus (8 lines). It gets its clock and reset from the circuits of the first two chapters. The address decoder selects which chip answers an access: the ROM, the RAM or one of the four IO chips. The IO chips connect the computer to the outside world: the ACIA to the PC, the first VIA to the display and the LED, both VIAs to the keyboard, and the SID to the speaker. The interrupt lines of the ACIA and the VIAs are wired together and lead to the processor.

From Breadboard to Finished Device

The breadboard is good for learning and trying things out. For a device you want to use, the circuit goes onto a printed circuit board. The board of this computer measures 160 × 100 mm and is fully populated. If you make it yourself, you must solder about 200 vias by hand. Schematics and board layout are in the hardware repository, as Eagle files and as a KiCad project, plus the Gerber files for manufacturing.

Two things are new compared to the breadboard:

  • The 9 V for the SID comes from a small boost converter module (5 to 9 V) on the board. On the breadboard an external source was enough.
  • The battery: a LiPo cell with a charging module and a boost converter supplies the computer with 5 V. This makes it portable.

The case is 3D printed and consists of a top and a bottom part. The top carries the display, keyboard and speaker; the bottom holds the boards and the battery. The designs are STL files in the hardware repository. To put the finished computer into operation, use the User Manual.

Continuing with Software

  • Write in C. The cc65 tools include a C compiler. You need your own start-up code that sets up the stack, the RAM and the zero page, plus the cc65 system library.
  • The monitor. The program from chapter 7 can be extended: an assembler and a disassembler, display of the processor registers, breakpoints, single-stepping, loading and saving programs over the serial interface.
  • BASIC. Microsoft BASIC runs on this computer. How to use it is described in the User Manual.
  • Test without hardware. The firmware repository of the project contains an extension for the MAME emulator that models the computer: processor, ROM, RAM, ACIA, VIAs, LCD and keyboard. You can try programs with it before you burn the EEPROM.
  • Use interrupts. The ACIA with receive interrupt, a system tick from a VIA timer, music from the timer of the second VIA: the circuit is already there.

Ideas for Extensions

The board is full, and the port lines of the VIAs are used up. Still, there are ways to extend the computer:

  • SD card. If you replace the 4-bit display connection with a small I²C adapter, at least four port lines become free. That is enough for an SD card reader. I²C also lets you add other chips, such as a real-time clock. You write the I²C protocol in software.
  • Keyboard with a counter chip. A decade counter (Johnson counter) can step through the keyboard rows, so that not every row needs its own port line.
  • Expansion connector. Many home computers of the seventies and eighties had a connector that brought address, data and control lines to the outside. The HomeComputer has none. You can add one with an extra board between the socket and the processor: it carries pins pointing down and a new socket for the processor on top, and so gives access to all signals.
  • More IO chips. Four outputs of the 74LS137 decoder (Y4 to Y7) are still free. You can connect four more chips to them.
  • Graphics. For video output you would have to plan a graphics chip and a larger board.

When Something Does Not Work

Troubleshooting is the most unpleasant part of hardware and software if you do not know what is going wrong. This order has proved useful:

  1. Supply: Measure +5 V between VCC and GND at every IC. At the SID also the 9 V.
  2. Clock: Do you see 1 MHz at the processor (pin 37)? Is PHI2 running at pin 39?
  3. Reset: Does /RESET stay Low briefly after power-on and then go High?
  4. Address bus: Are the address lines toggling? If A0 to A2 do not toggle, the processor is stuck.
  5. Chip select: Exactly one chip may be selected in each clock cycle. Two selected chips drive the data bus at the same time and disturb each other.
  6. Data bus: Pull suspicious chips one at a time (with the power off) and check whether the behaviour changes.
  7. The last step: Suspect the stage you built last first, and the wires there. On a breadboard, loose contacts and swapped wires are the most common cause.
  8. Simple programs: A program that only loops, or the monitor from chapter 7, shows whether the hardware reaches the processor before you suspect BASIC.

Good tools help: a multimeter, an oscilloscope and especially a logic analyzer with at least 16 channels, on which you see the address and data lines at the same time.
