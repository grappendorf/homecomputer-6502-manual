We have a clock and a reset, now comes the processor. There is still no memory and no input or output. Even so, its work can be measured, because we play a trick on it: it reads only a single instruction.

The 6502

The MOS 6502 is an 8-bit processor with 40 pins. It has 16 address lines (A0 to A15) and thus 64 KB of address space, eight data lines (D0 to D7) and an R/W line that shows whether it reads or writes. In addition there are the clock, reset, interrupt inputs and a few control lines. The following pins appear in the circuit:

Note

This description applies to the classic 6502. With CMOS successors such as the 65C02 some pins are assigned differently. Check the pinout in the datasheet in that case.

The idea: a memory full of NOPs

After reset the processor reads the addresses $FFFC and $FFFD, the reset vector, and starts the program at the address found there. It reads an instruction at that location and executes it. One of them does exactly nothing: NOP, code $EA.

If we wire the data lines to always deliver $EA, the processor behaves as if there were one huge memory containing nothing but $EA. It reads $EAEA as the reset vector and starts at address $EAEA. It reads $EA and executes NOP. Then it increments the program counter to $EAEB, reads $EA again and so on, until the program counter overflows and starts over.

Since the program counter is incremented by one after every NOP, we should measure square waves on the address lines whose frequency is halved from line to line.

The circuit

The eight data lines are connected either to +5 V or to GND. Together they form the value $EA = %11101010: D0, D2 and D4 to GND, all others to +5 V. This is permissible because the processor only reads here. It would be safer to put a resistor into each line.

The four inputs RDY, /IRQ, /NMI and /SO are each tied to +5 V through a 3.3 kΩ resistor. That is the idle level: the processor runs, and there is neither an interrupt nor an overflow signal. C6 (100 nF) decouples the supply. The pins PHI1 and PHI2 (pins 3 and 39) are outputs that only become important with memory chips.

Assembly

  1. Switch the power off. Run +5 V and GND from the first to the second breadboard, and connect the upper rails to the lower ones there.
  2. Insert IC3 across the channel. The 6502 is wider than the small ICs: its pin rows are six holes apart and therefore sit in rows d and h. The notch points left, pin 1 sits in hole 4d. Align all legs first: they stand slightly splayed and can be bent straight on the table. Press down evenly.
  3. Power IC3: pin 8 (11a) to +5 V, pin 1 (4a) and pin 21 (23j) to GND. Place C6 between the lower rails.
  4. Fix the data bus with eight wires from the holes of the upper row j to the upper rails. To GND: D0 (11j), D2 (13j) and D4 (15j). To +5 V: D1 (12j), D3 (14j), D5 (16j), D6 (17j) and D7 (18j).
  5. Place the four pull-up resistors: R6 at RDY (hole 5c), R9 at /IRQ (7c) and R8 at /NMI (9c), each from there to +5 V. Place R7 at /SO from hole 6i to +5 V.
  6. Connect the clock: hole 10a on the first breadboard to pin 37 (hole 7j) of the processor.
  7. Connect /RESET: hole 11g on the first breadboard via 13c and 13a to pin 40 (hole 4j) of the processor.
  8. Check everything once more before you switch the power on.

Measuring

Channel 1 shows the 1 MHz clock, channel 2 the line A0. A NOP takes two clock cycles: in the first the instruction is fetched, in the second it is executed. So A0 changes every 2 µs, and the signal has 250 kHz. Every further address line halves the frequency:

Note

No signals? Check one after the other: is +5 V at pin 8 and GND at pins 1 and 21? Does the clock run at pin 37? Does pin 40 go high after about half a second? And are the eight data lines correct?
